**Facet:** f4-sample-items · **Date:** 2026-11-22 · **Role:** research findings only (not a final report)

## 1. Source landscape and authority

- The Philippine regulator does **not** appear to publish released LET items with keys in the places found. The PRC-hosted March 2019 "English" PDF is a room/seat assignment list (candidate names, building, room, seat numbers, reporting reminders) with no test questions or answer key [22]. Treat any "PRC reviewer" as third-party unless a genuine released form is located.
- The clearest self-label came from a live quiz site: "These are practice materials, not official PRC exam questions"; it displays 30 questions and reveals score, correct answers and explanations only after submission [1].
- Current commercial reviewers organize banks by the PRC Table of Specifications: LETPass lists 22 English-major topics "from the PRC table of specifications," 4,110 questions, answer key plus rationale after answering, and 150-question/3h30m mock exams [3]; LET360 lists 1,100+ questions in 49 topic clusters and says each answer has an explanation plus "notes on why each wrong choice is wrong" [2].
- A large free PDF reviewer (`LET English Specialization Reviewer, Sets 1–10`) advertises 1,500 multiple-choice items with an answer key at the end of every set [4]. Its keys were verified in the downloaded text (see below).

**Implication:** model authentic items on third-party reviewers and PRC TOS topic labels, but do not claim official provenance.

### 2c. Phrasing and difficulty observations

- **Typical stem forms:** direct "Which of the following…?", completion with a blank, "Identify the author/work," quote-plus-interpretation, and classroom scenarios naming a teacher ("Teacher Arkin…", "Mr. Cruz…", "Margaux…"). Authors' names and school contexts are normal [4].
- **Comprehension is explicitly layered** in at least one live reviewer: literal vs inferential vs critical questions are separate items, even for the same poem [1].
- **Difficulty comes from terminology and discrimination, not long calculations**: items ask for a single technical term (inflectional morphemes, C-cloze vs cloze, carrier content, diminishing vs linear syllabus) or the best pedagogical action among four plausible ones [4].
- **Topic mix** in current banks is broad: literature, linguistics/structure, macroskills, material development, assessment, campus journalism, research and policy [2][3].
- Real reviewers contain **item-writing defects**: negative "NOT/EXCEPT" stems, "All of the above," uneven option lengths, typos ("followingg"), awkward grammar ("What is the oldest Indian documents…", "ensure the consistency"), and at least one usage item whose stem/options were split across lines in the PDF. Do not treat published reviewers as a quality model.

### 2d. Measured characteristics of the 1,500-item PDF [4] (computed from the extracted text)

- Key distribution across all 1,500 keys: **A 366, B 373, C 377, D 362, N/P 22** — close to balanced, but note the 22 "N/P" entries.
- **67** item stems contain "NOT" or "EXCEPT"; **33** options are "All of the above."
- **14** placeholder entries state that the item "was not included in the supplied source text" with no options; these correspond to most of the 22 N/P keys (e.g., set 1 item 97, set 5 items 132–135, set 6 items 96 and 128–135, set 8 items 117–124). A bank importer must drop N/P and placeholder rows.
- Deduplication matters: of **1,644** extracted stems, **1,605** were unique; **38** stem texts appeared more than once (**77** items in duplicate groups), e.g., the Rig Veda fact recurs in set 4 and set 10, and a "point of view assumed by the narrator" stem appears three times.

## 3. Evidence-based guidance for writing MCQs

- **Haladyna & Downing's item-writing rule taxonomy** ( reproduced by Penn State; **1989 — older source, still the standard checklist**): base each item on an objective; one problem per item; vocabulary at the examinee's level; items independent (no item cues another); no textbook-verbatim phrasing; no opinion-based items; use MCQ for higher-order thinking; stem in question or completion form (no blank at the start/middle); positive wording; no window dressing; options logical/numerical order, independent, homogeneous and similar in length; avoid "all/none of the above" and "I don't know"; avoid test-wise clues (clang associations, absurd options, absolutes like *always/never*, faulty grammar); key position roughly balanced; one correct option; plausible distractors built from common student errors, from true-but-irrelevant statements, and from familiar-but-incorrect phrases [7].
- **American College of Surgeons guidelines (accessed April 2020 — older):** clear unambiguous stems/options; items independent; stem as a complete question or completion; no "Which of the following is true/false" or "All… EXCEPT" lead-ins; no more than four options; one best answer; distractors parallel in form, similar in length, consistent in grammar/tense with the stem, plausible, mutually exclusive and free of clues; word the stem positively; the learner should be able to answer with the options covered [6].
- **Michigan OEAA item-writer training (2008 — older):** 18 practical rules including measure understanding rather than trivia, concise language, no trick/cute items, no bias, "According to the author" for inference items, avoid "What do you think?", no negative stems, options parallel in grammar/logic/length, plausible distractors, one clearly best answer, no "all/none of the above," numeric options ordered, and multicultural contexts (unfamiliar names in easy items, familiar names in hard ones). Its "six criteria for valid items": content matches expectation, required performance matches the expectation's verb, item cannot be solved by test-wiseness, one expectation at a time, important content, appropriate and engaging context; it also maps items to Depth-of-Knowledge levels [10].
- **NCME's assessment bibliography (2010 — older)** records that a taxonomy of **31** MCQ guidelines was validated against 27 textbooks and 27 research reviews/studies, and summarizes empirical consequences: distractor similarity to the key raised item difficulty by about **.12**; flawed items were **0–15 percentage points harder** than sound items, with a median **3.5-point** passing-rate difference; complex Type K items add clueing and should be avoided [9].
- **eCampus Ontario (current teaching guide):** MCQs are quick to deliver and give prompt feedback; provide **3–4 options** so the item is neither easy to guess nor slow to read, keep options of similar difficulty, let the stem provide a cue, and show feedback and correct errors promptly [14].

**Consolidated rule set for original LET-style items:** one TOS-aligned objective per item; 4 options; neutral, positively worded stem answerable before seeing options; homogeneous, mutually exclusive, parallel and similar-length distractors drawn from common learner errors; no "all/none of the above," absolutes, or cross-item clues; balanced key positions; run a test-wise/grammar-clue and duplicate check before publishing [7][6][10].

Provide a short rationale for the key and for each distractor [14].

## 4. Hints / clues during practice tests

### 4a. Hint strength: strong hints can hurt learning

- Two experiments on cue strength found the strongest cues produced the **fastest initial recall but the worst final-test performance**, while participants rated the strong-cue trials as most effective and most fun — an "illusion of learning" [20][21]. The primary paper is Vaughn et al., *The effect of hint strength on the benefits of retrieval practice* (Experiments 1 n=41, 2 n=32; consonants-only vs vowels-only vs no hint) [21]. **The publisher page for the primary paper timed out during extraction and is listed as a gap below.**
- In a separate pharmacy study, giving the **first three letters** of the answer as a hint significantly reduced long-term retention (d = .22) and more elaborative context did not improve retention [19].
- Practical translation: a clue button should give a **weak** cue (topic, strategy, first letter, "eliminate two options") rather than reveal the answer or most of it. Do not assume a clue that makes the item feel easier is helping [20][21][19].

### 4b. Fading cues and scaffolding

- Diminishing-cues retrieval practice (DCRP) starts with fuller cues and progressively removes them; it matched standard retrieval practice when testing worked well and **substantially outperformed it when initial learning was weak** [24].
- An evidence synthesis gives the same conditional design rule: for learners who would fail most attempts, give a brief study pass or cues first so attempts mostly succeed, then **withdraw cues** as success rises (free recall rather than recognition, fewer letter cues), because less support during practice produced better retention when learners could still retrieve [23][13].
- This is the strongest rationale for a *limited*, *graduated* clue button: offer a small number of escalating hints per item rather than one all-revealing hint [24][23].

### 4c. Feedback is not optional for MCQs

- With MCQs, uncorrected wrong options can be learned as facts; corrective feedback after each attempt removes lure intrusions, and immediate vs same-session delayed feedback were equally effective in one experiment [23]. The same synthesis reports quizzing with corrective feedback at g = .537 versus .374 without across classroom studies, and near-zero benefit when initial success was ≤50% with no feedback [23].
- Teaching guidance agrees: show feedback on MCQs and correct errors and missing information as soon as possible [14].
- Keep the practice low-stakes and "difficult but successful"; high stakes and strong hints both undermine learning, and effortful retrieval is the goal [12][20].

### 4d. Design implications for a 5–10-use clue button (inference, not a tested product spec)

1. Cap clues per session (5–10) **and** per item (e.g., 1–3 escalating levels) — scarcity alone is not evidence-based, but it discourages treating clues as a free answer key [20][21].
2. Level 1 = topic/strategy nudge; level 2 = eliminate one or two distractors or give the first letter; **do not** supply the answer or verbatim rule [20][19].
3. Require an answer attempt before releasing a clue; reveal rationale feedback after the attempt, whether correct or wrong [23][14].
4. Fade: once a learner answers a topic correctly, stop offering clues on it [24][23].
5. Log clue use per item and per topic; a high clue rate is a mastery signal, not merely a help request [23].
6. No published source found that tests a 5–10 clue allowance specifically, or a clue button in a Philippine LET mobile app — treat the cap as a product default to validate, not a research finding.
